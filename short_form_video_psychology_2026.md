# Human psychology for Reels, Clips, Shorts and vertical video

**Research through 15 November 2026.** A creator’s reference for writing an opening, directing attention on a phone, editing speech and action, teaching a skill, earning trust and deciding what to measure. “Clips” includes excerpts from podcasts, interviews, demonstrations and livestreams. The sources identify which findings come from TikTok, Kuaishou, Vine, mobile ads, film and classroom research.

**Scope.** This is a targeted evidence map. The published literature and GitHub have no complete register of every principle relevant to a vertical video. The selection prioritizes inspectable studies, quantitative syntheses, direct audience research, null findings and repositories with a clear research use. The examples throughout are proposed edits to test with your own audience.

**Contents.** 116 principles and research findings in 10 topic areas, 116 research and methods references, 13 official or institutional resources, and 15 public GitHub repositories.

## Start with the outcome

| If you want viewers to… | First edit worth testing | What would count as success? |
|---|---|---|
| Stop on a useful tutorial | Put the visible symptom and the affected object in the opening frame. | More eligible viewers stay, and first-time viewers identify what the clip will solve. [V02] [V06] [O02] |
| Understand a small on-screen change | Label the object beside the action at the moment it changes. | Viewers can identify or reproduce the change on a different example. [R09] |
| Follow a podcast or stream excerpt | Keep the question, identify the speaker and preserve the qualification that gives the answer its meaning. | A viewer new to the source can paraphrase the speaker’s claim accurately. [R38] [R40] [V21] |
| Remember a technique tomorrow | Ask for a prediction, demonstrate the answer, then ask for recall on a later example. | Correct unaided recall or task performance after a delay. [R24] [R25] [R26] |
| Share a clip with a specific person | Make the takeaway usable for a recipient with a recognizable problem. | Shares per a declared denominator, plus a sample of viewers’ reasons for sending it. [R44] [R45] |
| Trust a demonstration or review | Show the conditions behind the result, any sponsorship and the strongest limitation. | Viewers understand what was tested, who paid and where the conclusion applies. [R52] [R61] [R70] |

## Studies worth reading first

| Study and setting | What was actually found | Where the inference stops |
|---|---|---|
| **Mulier et al.** Three mobile advertising studies | Upright video could reduce the effort of watching on an upright phone and improve processing fluency. | Device posture, an ad stimulus and the available screen matter. A portrait crop that makes text unreadable solves a different problem. [V01] |
| **Zannettou et al.** 347 TikTok donors, 9.2 million video views | The authors estimated that 45% of views reached the end. Their measure used viewing duration relative to clip duration. | This was one donor sample and a behavioral proxy. It supplies neither a target completion rate nor a measure of what viewers understood. [V04] |
| **Violot et al.** 9.9 million Shorts and 6.9 million regular YouTube videos from about 70,000 channels | The formats differed in topic mix and in views, likes per view and comments per view. Their relative performance varied by category. | The observation period was 2021–22. Channel selection, video topic and later metric changes constrain comparisons with current uploads. [V05] [O03] |
| **Molem et al.** Two-week diaries and interviews with 12 TikTok or Reels users | Participants described encountering influential videos by chance, looking to comments for cues and sometimes trusting approachable experts with limited checking. | Self-reported experiences illuminate individual decisions. They cannot estimate the proportion of all viewers whose beliefs changed. [V06] |
| **Xiao et al.** 2,511 TikTok product promotions from 60 sellers | Shot count and visual complexity showed nonlinear associations with engagement. Speech rate related differently to sharing and to other responses. | Observational advertising data collected in 2020–21. The paper appeared online in 2024 and in a 2026 issue. No universal cut interval follows. [V08] |
| **Novanská Škripcová (2026).** Eye tracking, 16 people and ten TikTok videos | Different clips produced concentrated, alternating or fragmented gaze. Split-screen viewing could involve orderly alternation. | This small exploratory study measured gaze and physiology. It did not establish an optimal layout or a sales, comprehension or retention effect. [V09] |
| **Fang et al.** Randomized crossover with 64 dental students | Reels scored higher on several self-reported dimensions. Preferences also differed by year of training. | Perceived learning and efficiency were ratings. Claims about clinical competence require an objective task. [V16] |
| **Noetel et al.** Overview of 29 reviews covering 1,189 multimedia-learning studies | Signaling, spatial and temporal alignment, and captioning second-language video were among the strongest findings in the synthesis. | Outcomes concerned learning. Distribution, likes and organic reach need their own tests. [R09] |
| **Three research teams.** Feed interruptions and prospective memory | Under particular viewing and switching conditions, people were less likely to carry out a previously formed intention on cue. The 2026 studies also varied the cue conditions. | This tests remembering to do something after an interruption. It does not establish lasting harm from an individual clip or from a shot change. [V10] [V11] [V12] |
| **Nguyen et al. (2025) and Arouch et al. (2026).** Reviews of short-video use and cognition | The first review covered 71 studies and 98,299 participants. The second included 17 studies of adults aged 18–29 and highlighted heterogeneous findings and possible bidirectionality. | Heavier or problematic use can be correlated with poorer outcomes. Baseline differences and frequent use of self-report limit causal claims. The reviews may include overlapping studies. [V13] [V14] |
| **Chen et al. (October 2026).** Preregistered observational preprint | Of 502 participants, 398 had device-reported TikTok or Instagram use in the key analysis. Use was associated with poorer recognition of infrequent items and showed no association with the sustained-attention measure. | Screen time on an app can include other activity. The work was a preprint at the cutoff and cannot establish the causal direction. [V15] |

## Reading the evidence labels

**Format experiment** refers to an intervention involving short-form or mobile video. **Observed in feeds** covers logs, surveys, diaries, field correlations and exploratory measurements of actual short-video stimuli. **Format analysis** and **Format methods** identify platform analysis and measurement validation. **Video experiment** involves relevant video stimuli without assuming they appeared in a vertical Shorts feed.

**Transfer** marks evidence from another task, such as classroom learning, film, advertising or headline choice. **Framework** identifies a theoretical explanation whose individual predictions can differ in support. **Access guidance** identifies an accessibility standard or synthesis. **Preprint** identifies research awaiting peer review at the cutoff. The label describes the evidence nearest to the claim. All editing examples are applications. Research and method references are linked by ID, with full titles in the source index.

### The opening and the decision to stay

1. **Viewing motives.** People open a short-video feed for different reasons, including entertainment, escape, novelty and information. The motives for watching, sharing and making videos can differ.  
    *In the edit* — Decide whether this clip solves a problem, delivers a joke or invites participation. Let the first scene serve that motive. *Observed in feeds.* [V03] [V19]

2. **Incidental discovery.** Viewers can encounter a clip without searching for its subject or knowing the creator. In diaries, this was a common route to encountering ideas that changed a participant’s view.  
    *In the edit* — Give cold viewers a noun they can place. Replace “This changes everything” with “This setting changes how your phone records speech.” *Observed in feeds.* [V06]

3. **Goal-directed attention.** What someone is seeking influences which features they notice.  
    *In the edit* — For a beginner tutorial, show the symptom they recognize before showing your menu path. Ask a target viewer what problem they expect the clip to solve. *Transfer.* [R01]

4. **First impressions.** Features in the opening seconds predicted engagement in a study of very short Vine videos. Its six-second format shaped the result.  
    *In the edit* — Compare an opener showing the failed result with one showing the finished result. Keep the remaining edit identical. *Observed in feeds.* [V02]

5. **Orienting to a new onset.** An abrupt visual appearance can attract attention, depending partly on what the viewer is already attending to.  
    *In the edit* — Bring the missing screw into frame when its absence matters. Reserve the motion for that moment. *Transfer.* [R01] [R02]

6. **Habituation.** A repeated, inconsequential stimulus can evoke a weaker response with repetition.  
    *In the edit* — If every sentence has the same zoom and chime, remove them from routine lines. Keep one cue for the genuine change. *Transfer.* [R03]

7. **Information-gap curiosity.** Curiosity can arise when viewers know enough to recognize a specific piece of missing information.  
    *In the edit* — “Both exports used the same clip. Why is the second file half the size?” Show the setting that answers it in this video. *Framework.* [R20] [R21]

8. **Specificity of a tease.** In headline experiments, the effect of adding detail depended on how concrete the alternatives already were. Both vagueness and revealing everything can undermine selection.  
    *In the edit* — Test “Why the last subtitle vanished” against “Why the last subtitle vanished on export.” Measure selection and whether the promised answer satisfied viewers. *Transfer.* [R22] [R83]

9. **Interest and manageable novelty.** An unfamiliar idea can be interesting when people believe they can make sense of it.  
    *In the edit* — Introduce an unfamiliar waveform beside a familiar sound. Ask a novice to predict which peak is the clap. *Transfer.* [R23]

10. **Surprise and expectation violation.** A discrepancy can attract attention when the audience has an expectation to violate.  
    *In the edit* — Establish that two clips look identical, then reveal a single consequential difference. Give viewers time to perceive it. *Transfer.* [R86]

11. **Self-reference.** People often remember information better when they relate it to themselves.  
    *In the edit* — Ask “Which of these two microphone setups looks like yours?” before demonstrating the trade-off. A personal question alone need not create relevance. *Transfer.* [R28]

### Composition inside a vertical frame

12. **Portrait orientation and physical effort.** Mobile ad experiments found easier processing when the video fit a phone already held upright. This evidence concerns viewing effort in a particular device context.  
    *In the edit* — Reframe the subject for portrait viewing. For a wide software interface, cut from an overview into the relevant control so its label is legible. *Format experiment.* [V01]

13. **Competition between content and interface.** In a small TikTok eye-tracking study, viewers distributed their gaze differently across layouts and interface regions.  
    *In the edit* — Preview with the real buttons, account name and captions visible. Move the score, price or speaker’s hands clear of those overlays. *Observed in feeds.* [V09] [O06]

14. **Figure–ground segregation.** A subject is easier to separate from a background with clear boundaries.  
    *In the edit* — Put a light backing behind a dark cable in a repair demonstration. Inspect the frame at phone size. *Transfer.* [R06]

15. **Visual crowding.** Nearby detail can interfere with recognition, especially away from the point of fixation.  
    *In the edit* — Crop a terminal to the relevant lines and leave room around the variable name. Ask someone to read it during playback. *Transfer.* [R93]

16. **Proximity.** Nearby elements tend to be perceived as belonging together.  
    *In the edit* — Place each price immediately beside its own product. Preview where a line wrap places the second price. *Transfer.* [R06]

17. **Similarity and visual grouping.** Elements that look alike can be grouped even when spatially separated.  
    *In the edit* — Use the same outlined shape for all inputs and a different shape for outputs. Keep that convention through the demonstration. *Transfer.* [R06]

18. **Common fate.** Elements moving together tend to be seen as a unit.  
    *In the edit* — Pin a tracking label to the moving part it names. A free-floating label can appear to belong to the background. *Transfer.* [R06]

19. **Gaze cueing and joint attention.** Another person’s gaze can orient attention toward a location. Effects depend on the task and conditions.  
    *In the edit* — Have the presenter look at the cracked hinge, then show it clearly. Check whether viewers identify the hinge without an extra arrow. *Transfer.* [R05]

20. **Inattentional blindness.** Viewers concentrating on a task can miss a visible, unexpected event.  
    *In the edit* — If viewers are counting passes, give the off-ball event its own replay. Test whether they can identify what changed without prompting. *Transfer.* [R04]

21. **Signaling.** A cue can help viewers select and organize the relevant part of a visual explanation.  
    *In the edit* — Add one outline around the exact toggle as it changes. Check whether viewers can find that toggle on a fresh screen. *Transfer.* [R09]

22. **Contrast and redundant coding.** Text luminance and cues beyond color affect who can read and distinguish information.  
    *In the edit* — Put captions over a stable backing. Mark errors with a word or shape as well as hue. WCAG’s normal-text contrast reference is 4.5:1, with stated exceptions. *Access guidance.* [O09] [O10]

23. **Color in context.** Color meanings depend on the task, learned associations and surrounding context.  
    *In the edit* — In a chart, label the red series “before” if that is what it means. Test the palette with the intended audience before assigning emotional significance. *Transfer.* [R64] [O10]

### Voice, captions, music and timing

24. **Synchronized captions.** Timed text gives access to speech and can support comprehension. Names, numbers and negation are especially costly transcription errors.  
    *In the edit* — Watch muted and check every proper noun, “can’t,” decimal and speaker change. Caption meaningful off-screen sounds such as a timer ringing. *Transfer + access.* [R11] [O08]

25. **Language matching.** Same-language captions can support listening and word recognition. Findings vary with language proficiency, material and task.  
    *In the edit* — For a Hindi-English tutorial, preserve what was spoken and the exact English UI labels. Offer a translation separately when the audience needs it. Test a technical sentence on a phone. *Transfer.* [R12] [R75]

26. **Visible speech in noise.** Lip and face movements can help listeners understand speech in noisy conditions.  
    *In the edit* — Keep the speaker’s mouth visible for a difficult product name and show the written name. Audition the cut on a phone speaker. *Transfer.* [R13] [R11]

27. **Meaningful gesture.** Gestures can convey spatial relationships and movements that are difficult to communicate verbally.  
    *In the edit* — Show the hand rotating the dial as you say “one quarter turn.” Make sure the portrait crop includes both the hand and the dial. *Transfer.* [R14]

28. **Prosodic grouping and emphasis.** Pauses, stress and intonation help listeners parse phrases and detect contrasts.  
    *In the edit* — Read “use the left port, then restart” with a phrase boundary after “port.” Stress “left” only if the choice of port matters. *Transfer.* [R16]

29. **Competition from song lyrics.** Words in the music can interfere with a verbal task, especially while viewers must read or follow speech. The size of the effect depends on the material.  
    *In the edit* — Compare instrumental and lyric versions under the densest instruction. Ask viewers to repeat that instruction. *Transfer.* [R15] [R74]

30. **Temporal expectation.** A regular pattern can help listeners anticipate when an event will arrive.  
    *In the edit* — In a rhythm tutorial, establish the pulse before demonstrating the offbeat. Check whether viewers can reproduce the timing. *Transfer.* [R18]

31. **Music and emotional appraisal.** Musical expectancy, learned associations and expressive cues can contribute to an emotional response. Responses vary with the listener.  
    *In the edit* — Try silence, a restrained cue and a more energetic cue against the same ending. Ask viewers what emotion the person in the clip seemed to express. *Framework.* [R17]

32. **Audio quality and credibility.** In experiments, poorer sound changed evaluations of identical research content and speakers.  
    *In the edit* — Record the same explanation with a clean microphone and with room echo. Compare comprehension separately from credibility ratings. *Transfer.* [R19]

33. **Playback speed and intelligibility.** A meta-analysis of lecture playback found increasing speed can lower test performance, with smaller costs at modest speeds. Dense instructions give viewers little time to recover.  
    *In the edit* — Trim dead time around the instruction first. Test normal and faster delivery on a new example in the target language. *Transfer.* [R34] [R12]

34. **Nonvisual access.** A silent gesture, a change of color or a number onscreen may be the only place an essential fact appears.  
    *In the edit* — Say “the reading fell from 18 to 12” while showing the meter. Check that the conclusion is understandable with the screen covered. *Access guidance.* [O08] [O10]

### Cuts, excerpts and story structure

35. **Continuity across a cut.** Matching the action or direction of attention can help viewers find the same event after a cut.  
    *In the edit* — Cut from the hand reaching toward a latch to a close-up continuing the reach. Check that the change of scale preserves orientation. *Framework.* [R07]

36. **Event boundaries.** Viewers segment activity into meaningful events as goals, characters or actions change.  
    *In the edit* — Start a new shot when the repair shifts from diagnosis to testing. Hold the moment that proves the test succeeded. *Transfer.* [R08]

37. **Situation models and context.** A viewer tracks who is involved, where and when events occur, and what someone wants. Isolated excerpts can lose those anchors.  
    *In the edit* — In a podcast or stream clip, introduce the question and identify the speaker before an answer beginning with “he” or “that.” Retain the qualification that follows the quote. *Transfer.* [R40] [V21]

38. **Causal coherence.** Events with meaningful causal connections are easier to organize and can be remembered as more central to a story.  
    *In the edit* — Show the loose contact, the interrupted signal and the repaired result. A viewer should be able to explain why the middle shot matters. *Transfer.* [R38]

39. **Suspense.** Knowing what is at stake while uncertainty about the outcome persists can create suspense.  
    *In the edit* — Show that the bridge model must hold a load before adding the final weight. Let the audience see the outcome clearly. *Framework.* [R39]

40. **Retrospective curiosity.** Revealing an outcome before its cause gives the viewer a different question to follow through the story.  
    *In the edit* — Open on a bent bracket, then reconstruct how it bent. This ordering is useful when the surprising result itself is easy to see. *Framework.* [R39] [R20]

41. **Juxtaposition and the Kuleshov effect.** The context around a face can alter the emotion viewers attribute to it.  
    *In the edit* — Keep a reaction paired with the event it responded to. In a constructed sequence, ask viewers what the reaction means before assuming the cut reads correctly. *Transfer.* [R41]

42. **Narrative transportation.** When people mentally enter a coherent story world, that experience can relate to attitudes and persuasion.  
    *In the edit* — Follow one person pursuing a concrete goal. Compare the story version with a factual summary on both comprehension and agreement. *Transfer.* [R36]

43. **Character identification.** Perceived similarity can support identification, with variation across types of similarity and stories.  
    *In the edit* — Give the protagonist a recognizable constraint, such as fixing a device with only the tools at home. Check whether the intended audience recognizes that situation. *Transfer.* [R37]

44. **Remembered peaks and endings.** Retrospective evaluations can give particular weight to intense moments and endings. The effect varies across experiences and measurement choices.  
    *In the edit* — End on the audible clean recording or the working device. Ask viewers the next day which moment stood out. *Transfer.* [R31]

45. **Serial position.** In free-recall experiments, position in a list affected memory. A connected narrative can behave differently from a list.  
    *In the edit* — For a list-style Short, test whether a crucial middle item is recalled. Repeat it in a meaningful recap if it is routinely lost. *Transfer.* [R87]

### Comprehension, learning and memory

46. **Working-memory load.** New information must be held and related while the video continues. The load depends on complexity and existing knowledge.  
    *In the edit* — For a three-step workflow, leave the current command on screen while explaining its arguments. Ask a novice to perform that step. *Transfer.* [R10] [R74]

47. **Spatial contiguity.** Learning can improve when a label and its referent are close together, reducing the need to search back and forth.  
    *In the edit* — Place “noise floor” next to the quiet section of the waveform rather than in a distant legend. *Transfer.* [R09]

48. **Temporal contiguity.** Explanations and corresponding actions are easier to connect when presented together.  
    *In the edit* — Time “turn off auto gain” to the actual toggle change. Review the cut once without pausing. *Transfer.* [R09]

49. **Coherence and seductive details.** An interesting but irrelevant element can compete with the information needed for learning.  
    *In the edit* — Compare the tutorial with and without unrelated gameplay underneath. Ask viewers to solve a fresh example after watching. *Transfer.* [R27] [R09]

50. **Complementary verbal and visual information.** A well-chosen picture and words can give people complementary ways to represent the same idea.  
    *In the edit* — Show the knot tightening while naming the loop that moves. Use a drawing where it explains a relationship that narration alone leaves ambiguous. *Transfer.* [R88] [R09]

51. **Pictorial superiority.** Distinct pictures can be remembered better than their names under particular encoding conditions.  
    *In the edit* — Show the actual silhouette of the connector that a novice must recognize later. Test recognition among similar connectors. *Transfer.* [R89]

52. **Verbal redundancy.** Extra text can compete with a narrated diagram when viewers must process multiple verbal streams. Captioning needs and language proficiency change this trade-off.  
    *In the edit* — Keep usable captions. Remove the extra promotional paragraph sitting alongside the diagram while narration and captions are already present. *Transfer.* [R09] [R11] [R12]

53. **Segmentation and pacing.** Breaking complex material into meaningful parts can help learning. A learner-controlled pause and an editor’s cut are different interventions.  
    *In the edit* — Build a natural pause or replay point after the first operation. For an advanced viewer, test whether that pause still helps. *Transfer.* [R09] [R10]

54. **Pretraining.** Knowing component names or functions in advance can make the later interaction easier to understand.  
    *In the edit* — Introduce the gate and the threshold on a still image before showing what happens as the audio enters the gate. *Transfer.* [R94]

55. **Worked examples and guided practice.** Seeing the solution steps can reduce unproductive search when learners are new to a procedure.  
    *In the edit* — Demonstrate one edit including the reason for each choice. Give the viewer a second clip with one step left to do. *Transfer.* [R10]

56. **Expertise reversal.** Guidance that helps a novice can become redundant for someone with a well-developed mental model.  
    *In the edit* — Compare an annotated beginner version with a lean version for experienced editors, and score task success in each group. *Transfer.* [R10] [V16]

57. **Pretesting and prediction.** Trying to answer before instruction can focus learning when accurate feedback follows, even if the first guess is wrong.  
    *In the edit* — Freeze before a program prints its output, give time to predict it, then run the code and explain the result. *Transfer.* [R24]

58. **Retrieval practice.** Attempting to recall information can strengthen later memory. Tests inserted into longer video lectures have also influenced attention and learning.  
    *In the edit* — End a tutorial by hiding the setting and asking viewers to retrieve its name. Check again later if retention is the goal. *Transfer.* [R25] [R32]

59. **Spacing.** Practice spread across time often supports longer-term retention better than the same amount practiced in one sitting.  
    *In the edit* — Revisit yesterday’s concept in a new clip or a later prompt. Compare next-day recall at equal total practice time. *Transfer.* [R25]

60. **Self-explanation.** Explaining why a step works can help a learner connect the steps, with effectiveness depending on the task and prompt.  
    *In the edit* — After the fix, ask “Why did this change remove the echo?” Reveal the mechanism and one plausible wrong answer. *Transfer.* [R25] [R26]

61. **Interleaving and discrimination.** Mixing related problem types can help learners distinguish which procedure applies. Effects depend on what is being compared.  
    *In the edit* — Alternate clipped audio and background hum, then offer a new sample and ask viewers which problem they hear. *Transfer.* [R25]

62. **Informative feedback.** Feedback works differently depending on its content. Naming the error and how to correct it gives a learner something to act on.  
    *In the edit* — Replace “wrong” with “that slider changes volume. The threshold controls when the gate opens.” *Transfer.* [R26]

63. **Distinctiveness and memory.** One distinctive item can stand out in memory when its difference is meaningful relative to the rest of the set.  
    *In the edit* — In a sequence of valid examples, isolate the single case where the rule fails. Ask which case was exceptional. *Transfer.* [R35]

64. **Feeling of learning.** A smooth explanation can feel easy to learn while producing a different result on an objective test.  
    *In the edit* — Collect an ease rating and a separate “do it on a new clip” score. Treat a discrepancy as information about the lesson. *Transfer.* [R33] [V16]

### Emotion, humor and what people remember

65. **Arousal and sharing.** High-activation emotions, including awe or anger, were linked to greater transmission in news research and related experiments. Valence alone missed part of the pattern.  
    *In the edit* — Compare a genuinely surprising demonstration with a calm explanation. Measure sends or shares per eligible viewer and the reaction it prompted. *Transfer.* [R44]

66. **Arousal and memory priorities.** Arousal can strengthen processing of what already has priority while lower-priority details get lost.  
    *In the edit* — In a dramatic sound comparison, display the one important number before replaying the loud example. Ask what number viewers remember. *Framework.* [R46]

67. **Benign violations in humor.** One account of humor predicts amusement when an expectation is violated in a way the audience also finds benign. Groups differ in what feels benign.  
    *In the edit* — Let a harmless prop break the visual pattern. Test whether the intended audience reads the situation as playful. *Transfer.* [R42]

68. **Humor and message recall.** A funny advertisement can attract attention and liking, while effects on brand or message outcomes differ.  
    *In the edit* — In a product clip, make the product’s actual limitation or benefit essential to the joke. Ask viewers separately what was funny and what the product does. *Transfer.* [R43]

69. **Threat and efficacy.** A credible threat paired with an effective, feasible response can motivate action in fear-appeal research.  
    *In the edit* — In a backup reminder, show the actual failure and one recoverable step. Test whether viewers know how to restore the file. *Transfer.* [R47]

70. **Negativity and selection.** Negative words influenced clicks in news-headline testing. This is a selection outcome, and part of the source archive has a published randomization correction.  
    *In the edit* — When a problem is real, describe it precisely. Compare the negative opener with a neutral one on usefulness as well as starts. *Transfer.* [R91] [R83]

71. **Nostalgia and connection.** Personally meaningful memories often involve close others and can evoke social connection. Familiarity with a reference differs by age and culture.  
    *In the edit* — Use a recognizable school or household sound with an audience that knows it. Ask what memory it brought back. *Transfer.* [R92] [R72]

72. **Evaluative conditioning.** Pairing a neutral cue with something people already like or dislike can affect how the cue itself is evaluated.  
    *In the edit* — For a recurring series, pair a distinctive end sound with the genuine resolution. Test recognition and liking independently over several episodes. *Transfer.* [R79]

73. **Processing fluency.** Ease of processing can influence liking and other judgments. A polished presentation can feel convincing even when the claim still needs evidence.  
    *In the edit* — Simplify a cluttered comparison while keeping the underlying numbers visible. Ask about both clarity and correctness. *Framework.* [R29] [R19]

74. **Appearance comparison.** Experimental TikTok work with young women found that the type of appearance-related content affected immediate body-image outcomes.  
    *In the edit* — For a fitness clip, show what the person can do, how they practiced and what support they used. If studying viewer impact, measure it directly. *Format experiment.* [V17]

### Trust, persuasion and accurate interpretation

75. **Source credibility.** Perceived expertise and trustworthiness both matter, and their effects depend on the message and audience.  
    *In the edit* — Name who ran the test, how it was done and where a viewer can check it. A job title alone leaves the evidence unclear. *Transfer.* [R52] [V06]

76. **Involvement and argument quality.** When an issue matters personally, viewers may scrutinize the strength of the argument more closely.  
    *In the edit* — In a buying guide, show the measurement conditions a serious buyer would ask about. Test whether viewers can explain why one option fits. *Transfer.* [R76]

77. **Two-sided refutation.** Meta-analytic work distinguishes merely mentioning an objection from addressing it with reasons. The persuasion effects differ.  
    *In the edit* — State the best relevant objection, show the counterexample and specify when the objection still applies. *Transfer.* [R70]

78. **Framing equivalent outcomes.** People can respond differently to equivalent descriptions of a decision.  
    *In the edit* — For a benchmark, express the outcomes on the same denominator, such as 9 successes and 1 failure out of 10. Ask what rate viewers understood. *Transfer.* [R65]

79. **Anchoring.** An initial value can influence later numerical judgments.  
    *In the edit* — For a before-and-after price or latency clip, use a real, representative baseline with the same units and conditions. *Transfer.* [R66]

80. **Vivid examples and base rates.** A memorable case can come to mind more easily than the underlying frequency warrants.  
    *In the edit* — If one dramatic failure opens the clip, show how often it occurred across the whole test. Ask viewers to estimate the rate afterward. *Transfer.* [R66]

81. **The illusory-truth effect.** Repetition can increase perceived truth. A 2026 synthesis documents the effect and factors that change it.  
    *In the edit* — If correcting a rumor, make the verified fact the recurring caption. Give a source and briefly explain why the original claim fails. *Transfer.* [R57] [R58]

82. **Continued influence and correction.** A correction sometimes leaves a gap in the viewer’s explanation of events. A credible replacement account can help.  
    *In the edit* — Show what actually caused the apparent result. A standalone “false” stamp gives the viewer little explanatory material. *Transfer.* [R58]

83. **Accuracy prompts.** Reminding people to consider accuracy can improve discernment in some sharing tasks.  
    *In the edit* — Before inviting someone to forward a surprising claim, show the original source and ask what evidence would verify it. *Transfer.* [R59]

84. **Psychological inoculation.** Short videos that explain a misleading tactic in advance improved recognition of such tactics in experiments, including a YouTube field study.  
    *In the edit* — Demonstrate false dichotomy using a harmless example, then ask viewers to identify it in a different example. *Video experiment.* [R60]

85. **Sponsorship recognition.** A disclosure can change recognition of persuasive intent. Effects on attitudes and trust vary.  
    *In the edit* — Identify a sponsorship where the recommendation is made. Ask whether a viewer can tell who provided the product and who financed the test. *Transfer.* [R61]

86. **Perceived authenticity.** Qualitative influencer research distinguishes a creator’s demonstrated interests and transparency from the appearance of spontaneity.  
    *In the edit* — Show the actual test conditions and explain a genuine trade-off. A deliberately shaky camera is a poor substitute for that information. *Transfer.* [R84] [V06]

87. **Neuroscience language and authority.** Neuroscience language can change perceived explanatory quality even when it adds little to the reasoning.  
    *In the edit* — If discussing a “dopamine hook,” ask what was measured and whether the neural claim explains the result. Use behavioral terms when the data are behavioral. *Transfer.* [R85] [R73]

### Sharing, communities and creator relationships

88. **Practical value.** Useful content was more likely to be transmitted in research on news articles. A viewer might send a clip because it solves another person’s problem.  
    *In the edit* — Make one instruction portable enough to send to a teammate. Include the precondition and the result in the excerpt. *Transfer.* [R44]

89. **Recipient relevance.** People evaluate information partly through what sharing it would mean for themselves and for others.  
    *In the edit* — Replace “tag a friend” with an identifiable use case, such as checking a microphone with a podcast co-host. Ask whom a viewer would send it to and why. *Transfer.* [R45]

90. **Environmental cues and later word of mouth.** Everyday cues can bring a topic back to mind and support later conversation, even after immediate novelty fades.  
    *In the edit* — Connect a backup tip to the recognizable moment of unplugging a drive. Test whether viewers remember it when that situation recurs. *Transfer.* [R77]

91. **Social proof and cumulative advantage.** In an experimental music market, visible popularity changed choices and made eventual winners harder to predict.  
    *In the edit* — For a comparison, show blind samples before revealing audience votes when you want an independent judgment. *Transfer.* [R48]

92. **Descriptive and injunctive norms.** What people commonly do and what a group approves can send different signals. Both mattered in field research.  
    *In the edit* — Avoid presenting a widespread mistake as a recommended norm. Show the behavior your community endorses and give an accurate frequency when known. *Transfer.* [R49]

93. **Comments as social interpretation.** In a small Reels and TikTok diary study, people used comments to judge credibility and compare their reactions with others.  
    *In the edit* — Pin a sourced clarification when a comment reveals a widespread misreading. Inspect the types of comments alongside the total. *Observed in feeds.* [V06]

94. **Parasocial familiarity.** Viewers can feel an ongoing, one-sided connection with a recurring presenter. A relationship across episodes differs from one moment of eye contact.  
    *In the edit* — Use a recurring host if continuity serves the series. Give first-time viewers enough context to follow the episode independently. *Transfer.* [R50]

95. **Imitation and remix participation.** TikTok research describes how reusable sounds, actions and formats organize participation and imitation.  
    *In the edit* — Show the repeatable move and leave one element open to variation. Credit the originating work and check whether remixes preserve the intended idea. *Format analysis.* [V07]

96. **Group affiliation and moral language.** In studied social networks, moral-emotional language diffused more strongly within some group boundaries. This concerns network diffusion and group context.  
    *In the edit* — When explaining a topic across communities, test whether an insider term travels with the same meaning to outsiders. *Transfer.* [R78] [R72]

97. **Feedback and expressed outrage.** Social rewards and perceived network norms can shape later expressions of outrage. Visible expression and privately felt emotion are different outcomes.  
    *In the edit* — When qualifying community response, distinguish corrections, questions and pile-ons. An increase in comment count can conceal a shift in tone. *Transfer.* [R71]

98. **Mere exposure and familiarity.** Repeated exposure can change familiarity and liking, with diminishing or reversing returns in some conditions.  
    *In the edit* — Keep one recognizable series cue while varying the substance. Ask repeat viewers about both recognition and fatigue. *Transfer.* [R30]

99. **Reciprocity.** Receiving help can affect willingness to respond, with effects depending on social context.  
    *In the edit* — Provide the usable reference or example before inviting feedback. Make the request optional and measure voluntary contribution. *Transfer.* [R69]

### Motivation, choices and calls to action

100. **Autonomy.** Self-determination research links autonomy-supportive conditions with motivation.  
    *In the edit* — Offer an action the viewer can choose, such as trying the sample on their own footage. Explain what they will get from it. *Framework.* [R53]

101. **Competence and self-efficacy.** Seeing a manageable path to success can affect whether a person believes they can perform a task.  
    *In the edit* — Start with the smallest genuine version of the skill. Show the expected output so viewers can tell whether their attempt worked. *Framework.* [R55] [R53]

102. **Relatedness.** People can be motivated by a sense of connection and belonging.  
    *In the edit* — In a local-language learning series, invite viewers to compare how their team names the same tool. Check whether newcomers feel able to participate. *Framework.* [R53] [V03]

103. **Implementation intentions.** Linking a situational cue to a concrete action can help translate a goal into action.  
    *In the edit* — “When you finish recording, play the first five seconds through headphones.” Follow up on whether the action was done. *Transfer.* [R54]

104. **Psychological reactance.** Controlling language can threaten perceived freedom and provoke resistance. Results vary across contexts and audiences.  
    *In the edit* — Compare “you must change this now” with a clear reason and a freely chosen next step. Measure willingness and perceived pressure. *Transfer.* [R51]

105. **Choice overload and complexity.** Across studies, more options did not produce a universal average penalty. Task difficulty and how options differ matter.  
    *In the edit* — Give one next step when a viewer is learning one task. Offer a small comparison when choosing among alternatives is the purpose of the video. *Transfer.* [R56]

106. **Goal proximity.** In reward-program studies, effort sometimes increased as people approached a goal.  
    *In the edit* — For a real three-step task, show which step is complete and what remains. Test progress on the task, separately from completion of the video. *Transfer.* [R80]

107. **Scarcity.** Scarcity cues can affect purchase intentions, with variation by type of shortage and product. The measured outcome was purchase intention.  
    *In the edit* — In an event clip, give the genuine seat count or exact closing date. Check whether the relevant viewers understand the constraint. *Transfer.* [R67]

108. **Reference dependence and loss aversion.** In studied choices, people evaluated changes relative to a reference point, and some losses weighed heavily. The size and direction depend on the decision.  
    *In the edit* — Show what the viewer currently gives up under each choice, such as storage versus image detail, with the same comparison unit. *Framework.* [R90] [R65]

109. **Stable context and habit.** Repeated action in a stable context can become easier to trigger. One exposure does not establish a habit.  
    *In the edit* — For a practice series, attach a small activity to an existing routine and measure voluntary practice across days. *Transfer.* [R62]

110. **Fear of missing out.** Fear of missing others’ rewarding experiences is a measured individual difference associated with patterns of media use.  
    *In the edit* — For time-sensitive content, communicate what changes and when. Measure whether viewers felt informed or pressured. *Transfer.* [R68]

### Feed behavior, switching and viewer agency

111. **Variable distribution of gaze.** A 2026 exploratory TikTok study found concentrated, alternating and fragmented gaze patterns across different kinds of videos. Multiple streams sometimes produced organized switching.  
    *In the edit* — Test a demonstration and a reaction panel first side by side, then sequentially. Ask viewers what happened in each panel. *Observed in feeds.* [V09]

112. **Visual complexity and pace.** In a field study of product promotions on TikTok, shot count and image complexity had nonlinear relationships with engagement. The sample, genre and outcomes constrain the inference.  
    *In the edit* — Compare a sparse edit with a moderately detailed one of equal length. Locate the cut where understanding changes before changing pacing everywhere. *Observed in feeds.* [V08]

113. **Digital switching and boredom.** Experiments found that jumping between or through videos can increase immediate boredom and reduce satisfaction under studied conditions. Switching feeds and cutting within a video are different events.  
    *In the edit* — Give an episode a satisfying stopping point. If you test a looping ending, collect satisfaction as well as replay counts. *Video experiment.* [V18]

114. **Prospective memory under interruption.** Short-feed experiments found that some switching conditions impaired remembering to carry out a previously set intention on a later cue. This is a specific memory task.  
    *In the edit* — For a clip people use mid-task, make the intended action recoverable with a readable final instruction or reference frame. *Format experiment.* [V10] [V11] [V12]

115. **Attention and episodic memory are distinct.** In an October 2026 preprint, heavier use was related to one recognition-memory outcome, while the authors found no association with their sustained-attention task in the device-use subgroup.  
    *In the edit* — Choose the outcome your claim names. A delayed recognition test and a time-watched counter answer different questions. *Preprint.* [V15] [V13] [V14]

116. **Reported exposure and measured behavior.** People can misestimate how often or how long they use short-video apps. Device logs capture usage time and still leave the viewer’s mental activity unknown.  
    *In the edit* — For an audience study, ask about frequency and duration separately. Compare with consented logs if those data matter to the question. *Format methods.* [V20] [V04]

## Claims that deserve a second look

| Common creator claim | What the evidence supports | A better test |
|---|---|---|
| “Humans have an eight-second attention span.” | The often-repeated number has no basis as a universal human limit. Attending to a search target, listening to a story and choosing whether to swipe are different tasks. [O13] [R01] | Name the task and audience. Measure the specific point at which people leave or miss the instruction. |
| “Hook everyone in three seconds and cut every two.” | Early features mattered on Vine. A study of TikTok sellers found nonlinear associations with shot count and complexity. Neither identifies a universal deadline or sequence of cuts. [V02] [V08] | Compare two equally long edits, using a meaningful reveal at different times. |
| “An unfinished loop forces people to return because of the Zeigarnik effect.” | A 2025 meta-analysis found no general recall advantage for unfinished tasks. It found a tendency to resume interrupted tasks under study conditions. Returning to a creator’s second video is another behavior. [R63] | Resolve the promised point. If making a series, compare voluntary starts of the next episode and satisfaction with the first. |
| “Every question makes a stronger hook.” | A prequestion before a lesson can aid learning. A 2026 study of written titles found lower engagement for question framing across several settings. Another registered report found that the value of specificity depended on the alternatives. [R24] [R95] [R22] | Compare question and statement versions carrying equal information. Measure both selection and learning. |
| “Every unexpected cut gives a dopamine hit.” | Reward-prediction-error evidence comes from particular neuroscience tasks. A cut, view or replay does not measure dopamine release. [R73] [R85] | Measure whether the viewer anticipated the reveal and whether it made sense afterward. |
| “Split-screen gameplay improves everyone’s attention.” | The 2026 TikTok eye-tracking study observed several gaze patterns. Learning research shows that irrelevant details can interfere with learning under some conditions. [V09] [R27] | Compare aligned and unrelated secondary panels on a comprehension question as well as viewing time. |
| “All captions should be one fast-moving word at a time.” | Captions can improve access and comprehension. The cited captioning research does not establish universal superiority for word-by-word animation. [R11] [R12] [O08] | Compare stable phrase captions with highlighted words, using sound-off comprehension and viewer reading comfort. |
| “Red creates urgency, blue creates trust, and either increases views.” | Color effects depend on context and audience. Contrast and an explicit label solve more clearly defined perceptual tasks. [R64] [O09] [O10] | Test whether viewers correctly identify the warning or comparison on the actual phone background. |
| “Longer watch time proves interest, learning or satisfaction.” | Duration, opportunities for replay and the platform’s counting rules affect viewing metrics. Digital switching can also leave viewers less satisfied. [R81] [O02] [O03] [V18] | Pair watch time with a task, a rating or a voluntary action tied to the actual claim. |
| “A more viral message must be better or more accurate.” | Exposure and visible popularity can amplify differences. Accuracy prompts can change sharing decisions, and repetition can increase perceived truth. [R48] [R57] [R59] | Score accuracy independently of reach. Where practical, evaluate the content before showing public counts. |

## Measuring a principle on a real video

| Outcome | Operational definition to use | What complicates interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Initial hold | Use the platform’s named hold or stayed-to-watch metric. For an experiment, define the eligible exposures and time threshold in advance. | The observed viewers depend on traffic source, personalization, placement and whether the item autoplayed. [O01] [O02] [O07] |
| First-pass retention at a moment | In your own instrumented player, divide eligible first plays reaching time *t* by eligible first plays. Document how seeking and incomplete logs are handled. | Platform curves may count loops, scrubbing and late entry differently. A spike can indicate interest, confusion or high replay value. [V04] [O02] |
| Duration and average percentage | Compare similar lengths and record whose views form the denominator. Eight seconds of a ten-second clip is 80%. Twenty-four seconds of a forty-second clip is 60%. | The second example produced more seconds and a smaller fraction. Neither alone establishes which served the viewer better. [R81] [O02] |
| Understanding and memory | Ask an unprompted paraphrase, a recognition question with plausible alternatives or a novel transfer task. Use a delayed test for a retention claim. | Repeating the exact example can measure recognition. A confidence rating measures a different construct. [R24] [R25] [R33] |
| Satisfaction and trust | Ask whether the clip delivered its promise and what evidence the viewer would use to verify the claim. Ask trust and correctness questions separately. | Ratings are affected by who responds, familiarity, audio quality and sponsorship recognition. [R19] [R52] [R61] |
| Sharing, saving and action | Declare the denominator, such as unique reached accounts or eligible impressions, and the time window. Use aggregate voluntary task completions when actual behavior is the goal. | A send can mean endorsement, disagreement or a private joke. A save does not demonstrate later use. [R44] [R45] [V06] |

**Platform definitions matter.** YouTube counts a Shorts view when the Short starts or replays under its method effective 31 March 2025. Its engaged-views metric excludes loops and reflects staying beyond the initial seconds. Studio’s average view duration and average percentage viewed for Shorts use the viewers who stayed. YouTube also describes satisfaction and personalization signals. Record the date and the label exported with your result. [O01] [O02] [O03]

**Instagram, TikTok and extracted clips.** Meta introduced Reels watch-time metrics and later described changes to plays, replays and retention reporting. Check the current in-app definition when comparing periods. TikTok’s ranking documentation discusses interactions and personalization. Its creative codes describe ad production. Twitch’s clipping tools describe how to extract and edit a moment. None of these documents is a randomized comparison of two edits. [O04] [O05] [O06] [O07] [O12]

**A small test you can actually run**

1. Pick one viewer decision and one primary outcome before editing. One testable prediction is “More first-time viewers will find the toggle in a new screenshot when the label appears beside it.” Record satisfaction as a secondary outcome. [R09] [R82]
2. Make matched versions. Hold the footage, script, duration, sound mix and audience constant except for the label placement. For a cold-viewing question, show only one version per person. Randomly assign consenting participants if possible. [R82]
3. Predefine the observation window, stopping rule, success threshold, exclusions and units. Forty starts by one viewer are one viewer’s behavior. Record sample sizes, missing responses and uncertainty. [R82]
4. Report the raw numerator and denominator, the absolute difference and an interval where possible. When variants were just posted on different days, call the difference observational and record traffic source, audience, timing and competing posts. [R82] [O01]
5. Replicate a promising change on another topic. Check a muted phone, a viewer unfamiliar with the series, and the languages you publish in. Region, reading proficiency, device and prior experience can materially change the result. [R11] [R72] [R75]

For an ad or visually intense clip, preview the actual app overlays and avoid rapid flashing. WCAG’s three-flashes-or-below-threshold criterion accounts for area, intensity and red flashes. For editors who do not analyze those thresholds, omitting strobing is straightforward. [O06] [O11]

## Search method and coverage

**Cutoff and selection.** The search includes work available online by 15 November 2026. Foundational work had no starting-year restriction. Unique cited works are counted once, including one explicitly marked October 2026 preprint and a data-archive correction. A 2026 publication can synthesize studies that ended several years earlier. The 2026 BMJ review, for example, searched the 2014–24 period. [V14] [V15] [R83]

**Where the search looked.** Targeted web searches covered PubMed and PMC, ACM proceedings and author copies, scholarly publishers, arXiv and institutional repositories. The search also covered official platform and W3C documentation, GitHub project pages, dataset documentation and correction notices. English-language queries can miss research indexed only in other languages, private platform experiments, unpublished null results and unindexed work. Some records supplied abstracts or author summaries rather than open full text. This was a targeted review without a preregistered search protocol or independent duplicate screening.

| Search family | Representative terms and combinations |
|---|---|
| Direct audience evidence | `("short-form video" OR TikTok OR "Instagram Reels" OR "YouTube Shorts" OR Vine) AND (experiment OR eye tracking OR data donation OR systematic review OR memory OR engagement)` |
| Phone format and clips | `vertical versus horizontal mobile video advertising processing fluency`, `TikTok shot count visual complexity engagement`, `creative livestream clips temporal segmentation` |
| Perception and learning | `multimedia learning meta-analysis signaling contiguity`, `same-language subtitles eye tracking India`, `captioned video L2 meta-analysis`, `cinematic continuity event segmentation`, `playback speed meta-analysis` |
| Narrative and persuasion | `narrative transportation meta-analysis`, `headline concreteness registered report`, `Zeigarnik Ovsiankina meta-analysis`, `emotional arousal social transmission`, `illusory truth 2026 meta-analysis`, `parasocial relationships meta-analysis` |
| Research software | `site:github.com KuaiRand OR KuaiRec OR MicroLens`, `watch time duration bias D2Co`, `Upworthy archive randomization correction`, `TikTok data donation research GitHub`, `behavioral experiments jsPsych`, `shot detection captions audio analysis GitHub` |
| Recency and contrary findings | Month- and year-filtered searches through the cutoff, null results, alternative outcomes, publication versus preprint status, dataset corrections and project-maintenance notices. |

**Inclusion decisions.** For psychological claims, priority went to direct short-video studies, informative replications, quantitative syntheses and foundational work with a well-defined mechanism. A platform document was used for its own definitions or design guidance. A repository was included when its documented data or method supported a named research task. Creator benchmarks without an inspectable sample, vendor claims about universal timing and generic “viral video” code lists were excluded from the evidence base.

**Gaps to carry forward.** Much direct evidence concerns TikTok or specific demographics. Classroom learning, headline clicks and purchases measure different behaviors. Reels and Shorts change their interfaces and selection mechanisms. Repeated views from one person do not represent independent people. Effects can change with phone size, genre, language, audience expertise, sound availability and whether someone chose to watch or was shown an autoplay item. Treat an exact timing, color, edit cadence or reach target as a local testable choice. [V04] [V05] [V09] [R72] [R75] [O01]
